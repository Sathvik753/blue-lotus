% --------------------------------------------------------------------- abstract
\begin{abstract}
\noindent
We report a walk-forward, out-of-sample validation of the Blue Lotus
stress-testing engine (release v3.1) across a panel of 62 liquid instruments
spanning seven asset classes and twelve annual test periods (2013--2024),
yielding 744 independent evaluation cells. For each asset and test year the
engine is estimated using only data available strictly before the test year,
and its predicted one-year maximum-drawdown distribution is compared against the
realized drawdown. This expanding-window design removes the single-period
weakness of a one-shot hold-out: every year is a genuine out-of-sample test, and
the panel spans multiple market regimes.

On the 496 cells drawn from non-crisis years, the engine's $5\%$ and $1\%$
lower-tail bounds are breached at $4.0\%$ and $1.0\%$ respectively---rates for
which a Kupiec proportion-of-failures test fails to reject nominal calibration
($p=0.31$, $p=0.99$; the test treats cells as independent, an assumption the panel
only approximately satisfies). We do not claim uniform calibration of the whole
distribution: a Kolmogorov--Smirnov test rejects PIT uniformity in every stratum,
and in non-crisis years the departure is conservatism---the median
probability-integral-transform (PIT) rank is $0.83$, so realized drawdowns are
typically milder than the engine's central prediction, the prudent direction of
error. Pooling all 744 cells the mean PIT rank is $0.53$, but this near-central
value masks two opposing effects---calm-year conservatism and crisis-year
breaches---that average out. The residual tail miss is concentrated in the four
crisis years (2015, 2018, 2020, 2022): there the $5\%$ breach rate rises to
$34.7\%$, degrading monotonically with severity and peaking in the March 2020
shock ($67.7\%$ breach, median PIT $0.09$). Against four baselines (Gaussian,
Student-$t$, and i.i.d.\ and block bootstraps) scored by the Continuous Ranked
Probability Score, the engine is the only model with non-rejectable calm-market
tail calibration and improves on every baseline's forecast skill by $20$--$39\%$
in non-crisis years, conceding sharpness only in crises---a deliberate trade the
baselines make in reverse by being over-dispersed in all regimes. The engine
therefore reproduces the frequency of ordinary tail losses faithfully out of
sample and localizes---rather than conceals---the residual gap that a
history-calibrated model cannot close.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} stress testing, walk-forward validation, out-of-sample
backtest, maximum drawdown, Kupiec test, probability integral transform,
calibration, tail risk.

% =============================================================== 1 Introduction
\section{Introduction and Motivation}

A stress-testing engine is only as credible as the evidence that its predicted
distributions actually contained what subsequently happened. A companion paper
specifies the engine's design and methodology in full and validates it on a
single crisis-versus-calm event study. That design has two limitations this paper
is written to remove.

First, an event study anchored to a fixed set of historical windows tests the
engine at hand-chosen points in time. A more demanding and more honest protocol
is \emph{walk-forward} (expanding-window) validation: for each test period, the
model is re-estimated using \emph{only} the data available before that period,
and evaluated on the period that follows. Repeating this across many consecutive
periods yields a panel of strictly out-of-sample tests that (i) enforces
train-before-test ordering everywhere, eliminating look-ahead, and (ii) spans
every regime the sample contains, rather than a single hold-out window whose
character---calm or turbulent---would otherwise dominate the verdict.

Second, the results reported here are produced by engine release \textbf{v3.1},
which corrects a set of systematic biases identified in an internal review of the
prior release. Every correction moved reported risk in the conservative
direction, so the calibration evidence below is measured on the corrected engine
rather than its more optimistic predecessor. Section~\ref{sec:engine} summarises
the changes.

The contribution is a single, large, reproducible calibration study: 744
out-of-sample cells across 62 instruments and 12 years, with proportion-of-failures
and distributional-uniformity tests, stratified by market regime and by asset
class.

% =============================================================== 2 Engine
\section{The Engine Under Test (v3.1)}
\label{sec:engine}

The numerical engine is unchanged in architecture from the companion paper: input
processing and winsorization, a deterministic three-state volatility-regime model,
Bayesian shrinkage of regime parameters, Generalized Pareto tail estimation via
Peaks-over-Threshold Extreme Value Theory, regime-switching Monte Carlo path
generation with a drawdown-conditional blend and a hard loss floor, an optional
Esscher importance-resampling step, and bootstrapped confidence intervals. Release
v3.1 corrects the following, all of which had biased reported risk optimistically:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em, itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Tail estimation on raw losses.} The Generalized Pareto fit now
  uses the unwinsorized loss series; winsorizing before the fit had bounded the
  exceedances by construction and forced a spuriously short tail. Winsorization is
  retained only for bulk-moment estimation (regime detection and shrinkage).
  \item \textbf{No path rejection.} An earlier step deleted the most extreme $1\%$
  of simulated paths before measurement; it is removed, so every path is retained.
  \item \textbf{Consistent Esscher units.} The tilt's mean and Expected-Shortfall
  constraints are now expressed in common daily-return units, so the change of
  measure can engage rather than silently reverting to the identity.
  \item \textbf{Row-aligned resampling.} Regime paths are reindexed together with
  return paths through any resampling step, so per-regime statistics pair returns
  with the correct regime sequence.
  \item \textbf{Compounded drawdowns.} Drawdowns are computed on compounded wealth
  and are bounded below by $-100\%$, on both the simulated and the realized side of
  every comparison.
  \item \textbf{Stop-outs, not deletion.} A client-declared risk limit freezes a
  path at its loss rather than removing it from the sample.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
For this study the engine is run with its documented production defaults:
winsorization at the $1$st/$99$th percentile for bulk moments; drawdown-conditioning
thresholds at $-5\%$ (moderate) and $-15\%$ (severe); Extreme-Value level
$\alpha=0.05$; $N=3{,}000$ Monte Carlo paths per cell; and a fixed random seed of
$42$. All drawdowns are one-year maximum drawdowns on compounded wealth.

% =============================================================== 3 Protocol
\section{Walk-Forward Protocol}

\subsection{Expanding-window design}
Let an instrument have daily returns $\{r_t\}$ with dates $\{d_t\}$. For a test
year $Y$, define the \emph{training set} as every observation strictly before the
first trading day of $Y$,
\[
  \mathcal{T}_Y = \{\, r_t : d_t < \text{$1$ January }Y \,\},
\]
and the \emph{test set} as the returns realized during $Y$. The window is
\emph{expanding}: $\mathcal{T}_Y$ grows as $Y$ advances, so each fit uses the
maximum admissible history---appropriate for a tail model, which is data-hungry
and has no reason to discard older crises. We require at least $504$ training
observations ($\approx 2$ years) and at least $200$ realized test days per cell;
cells failing either threshold are skipped.

The engine is estimated on $\mathcal{T}_Y$ and simulates a forward path ensemble
of horizon equal to the number of trading days in $Y$, so the predicted maximum-drawdown
distribution and the realized maximum drawdown span the same length. The realized
maximum drawdown $\dd^{\mathrm{real}}$ is computed on the raw (unwinsorized) test
returns, in the same compounded-wealth convention as the simulation.

\subsection{Coverage statistics}
For each cell we record the realized maximum drawdown $\dd^{\mathrm{real}}$ and the
simulated distribution $\{\dd^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^N$, and compute the
\emph{probability-integral-transform rank}
\[
  u \;=\; \frac1N \sum_{j=1}^{N} \ind\!\left[\dd^{(j)} \le \dd^{\mathrm{real}}\right],
\]
the simulated fraction at least as severe as reality. Under a correctly specified
model $u \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)$. A \emph{$p5$ (resp.\ $p1$) breach} occurs when
$\dd^{\mathrm{real}}$ is worse than the simulated $5$th (resp.\ $1$st) percentile.
For $x$ breaches in $n$ cells at nominal rate $p$, the Kupiec proportion-of-failures
statistic
\[
  \mathrm{LR}_{\mathrm{POF}}
  = -2\ln\frac{(1-p)^{n-x}\,p^{x}}{(1-\hat\pi)^{n-x}\,\hat\pi^{x}},
  \qquad \hat\pi = x/n,
\]
is asymptotically $\chi^2_1$. We additionally test the full PIT sample for
uniformity with a one-sample Kolmogorov--Smirnov statistic, and report the $90\%$
band coverage---the fraction of cells with $\dd^{\mathrm{real}} \in [p5, p95]$.

\subsection{Universe}
The panel comprises 62 instruments chosen for liquidity and long history, grouped
into seven asset classes: US equity beta (e.g.\ SPY, QQQ, IWM), sector funds
(the nine SPDR sectors), international equity (EFA, EEM, and country funds), rates
(TLT, IEF, SHY, AGG, TIP), credit (HYG, LQD, JNK), commodities (GLD, SLV, USO,
DBC), and a set of long-history single-name equities. Crossed with the twelve test
years, the grid yields $744$ evaluation cells. Four years are designated
\emph{crisis} years for stratification---2015 (China/oil), 2018 (Volmageddon and
the Q4 selloff), 2020 (COVID), and 2022 (the rate shock)---leaving eight
non-crisis years.

\subsection{Benchmark models and scoring}
\label{sec:benchmethod}
Calibration alone does not establish that the engine's structure earns its
complexity; a naive model could calibrate as well. We therefore evaluate four
baselines on the \emph{identical} cells, each fit on the same training window and
each producing an $N=3{,}000$ ensemble of one-year maximum drawdowns: (i) an
i.i.d.\ Gaussian fit---the textbook Monte Carlo the engine is designed to
improve on; (ii) an i.i.d.\ Student-$t$ fit, a fat-tailed parametric competitor;
(iii) a historical i.i.d.\ bootstrap of training returns; and (iv) a circular
block bootstrap of block length $20$, which preserves the volatility clustering
the regime model targets and is the strongest baseline. Beyond the breach and PIT
statistics, each cell is scored with the Continuous Ranked Probability Score,
\[
  \mathrm{CRPS}(F, y) = \E\,|X - y| - \tfrac12\,\E\,|X - X'|,
\]
a strictly proper scoring rule for a probabilistic forecast of a scalar (with
$X,X'$ independent draws from the forecast ensemble $F$ and $y$ the realized
drawdown; lower is better). We report mean CRPS, the engine's CRPS skill score
$1 - \overline{\mathrm{CRPS}}_{\mathrm{engine}} /
\overline{\mathrm{CRPS}}_{\mathrm{base}}$ (positive favours the engine), the share
of cells on which the engine's CRPS is lower, and a paired Wilcoxon signed-rank
test on the per-cell CRPS differences.

% =============================================================== 4 Results
\section{Results}

\subsection{Headline calibration}
Table~\ref{tab:headline} reports the panel. The central result is a
tail-\emph{frequency} statement, and we state it as exactly that. On the 496
non-crisis cells the $5\%$ bound is breached in $4.0\%$ of cells and the $1\%$
bound in $1.0\%$; a Kupiec proportion-of-failures test fails to reject nominal
calibration at either level ($p=0.31$ and $p=0.99$). This is a statement about
exception \emph{rates}, not about the shape of the whole distribution: as
Section~\ref{sec:uniform} shows, the engine is mildly conservative through the
body (non-crisis mean PIT rank $0.69$), so it over-predicts risk in calm markets
rather than under-predicting it---the prudent direction of error. Across the full
panel the mean PIT rank is $0.53$; this near-central average should not be read as
distributional calibration, because it pools the calm-year conservatism above with
the crisis-year breaches below (uniformity is rejected in every stratum,
Section~\ref{sec:uniform}). The overall breach rates ($14.3\%$ at $p5$) are a blend
dominated by the crisis subset, examined next.

The $90\%$ band coverage is near-identical across the two regimes ($63.5\%$ vs

\subsection{Distributional uniformity}
\label{sec:uniform}
The breach tests above concern exception \emph{rates}; a stronger requirement is
that the full PIT sample be uniform, and it is not. A one-sample
Kolmogorov--Smirnov test rejects uniformity in every stratum: overall $D=0.18$,
non-crisis $D=0.34$, crisis $D=0.49$, all with $p<10^{-3}$. These rejections have
opposite sources and must not be collapsed into a single verdict. In non-crisis
years the departure is \emph{conservatism}---the median PIT rank is $0.83$, so
realized drawdowns are typically milder than the engine's central prediction; the
model over-states body risk, which for a risk system is the prudent failure mode.
In crisis years the departure is the tail breach documented below (median PIT
$0.09$). The engine is therefore well calibrated in the specific sense the Kupiec
tail test measures---the \emph{frequency} of $5\%$ and $1\%$ exceedances in
ordinary markets---but it is not a uniform generator of the entire drawdown
distribution: it errs conservative through the body. We report this explicitly
rather than let the favourable breach rates stand alone.

\subsection{Calibration of predicted quantiles}
Figure~\ref{fig:reliability} plots empirical exceedance frequency against nominal
lower-tail probability across the whole panel. Through the interior the empirical
frequency runs at or below the nominal line---the graphical form of the body
conservatism just described---and it lifts above the line only in the extreme
lower tail, where the crisis-year breaches accumulate. The curve is thus the
picture of a model whose exception \emph{rates} are near nominal in the deep tail
once crises are pooled in, while sitting conservative elsewhere.

% =============================================================== 4b Benchmark
\section{Comparison Against Baselines}
\label{sec:benchmark}
Table~\ref{tab:benchmark} places the engine against the four baselines of
Section~\ref{sec:benchmethod}. The result is regime-dependent, and stating it
precisely is the point of the exercise.

In non-crisis markets---where a risk system operates the great majority of the
time---the engine dominates on both axes at once. It is the \emph{only} model
whose tail calibration is not rejected ($4.0\%$ $p5$ breach, Kupiec $p=0.31$);
every baseline breaches at $0.6$--$0.8\%$, six to eight times below nominal, which
means they systematically over-state tail risk and would raise near-constant false
alarms. That same over-dispersion costs them sharpness: the engine's mean CRPS is
$20$--$39\%$ lower than every baseline (paired Wilcoxon $p<10^{-3}$ throughout),
and it wins on $71$--$82\%$ of calm cells. Pooled over the full panel the engine
still posts the lowest CRPS, beating the Gaussian, Student-$t$, and i.i.d.\
bootstrap by $7$--$14\%$ and edging the block bootstrap by $0.6\%$ (a statistical
tie, Wilcoxon $p=0.04$).

In crisis years the ranking inverts: the engine's CRPS is $27$--$38\%$
\emph{worse} than the baselines, and it wins only about a quarter of crisis cells.
This is both honest and informative. The baselines score better in crises not
because they are calibrated---none is; all four fail the Kupiec test at
$p<10^{-3}$, with $p5$ breach rates of $21$--$22\%$---but because they are
uniformly over-dispersed \emph{everywhere}, so a permanently-too-wide distribution
happens to sit closer to a realized extreme. The very sharpness that wins the
engine the calm regime is what costs it in the tail. A model that is vague in all
weather looks better in a storm and worse the other four-fifths of the year; the
engine makes the opposite trade deliberately, and the operator-configurable crisis
overlay described in the companion engine paper is the lever for widening the
distribution when sizing for systemic scenarios.

% =============================================================== 5 Discussion
\section{Discussion}

The walk-forward panel delivers a sharper version of the message from the earlier
event study, and it does so on the corrected engine. On ordinary market
conditions---the regime in which a risk system spends the overwhelming majority of
its life---a Kupiec test fails to reject nominal $5\%$ and $1\%$ tail-breach rates
out of sample across 496 asset-years, with the body of the distribution biased
toward caution. We are careful about the strength of that claim: it concerns
exception frequencies, not the full distribution (which is not uniform,
Section~\ref{sec:uniform}), and the test assumes independent cells, which the
panel only approximately satisfies (Section~\ref{sec:limits}). Within those
bounds it is the precondition for taking any crisis result seriously: a model that
over-fired in calm markets would provide no standing to interpret its behaviour in
storms.

The residual miss is real, and the study measures rather than hides it. It is
concentrated in the crisis years, monotone in realized severity, and concentrated
in the exposures that carry systemic tail risk. The natural explanation is not
that the model had seen no crisis: price history begins in 2004, so every
training window for the 2020 test cells spans twelve to sixteen years and
\emph{includes the 2008 global financial crisis} without exception. The engine
under-covered 2020 despite having been fit on 2008. The operative fact is that a
tail calibrated to one systemic episode need not envelope the next: the March 2020
drawdown's velocity and cross-asset breadth differed from the 2008 shape the model
had learned, in exactly the dimensions that drive a one-year maximum drawdown. A
history-calibrated tail is bounded by the worst \emph{shape} it has observed, and
2020 exceeded 2008 on that axis. The design response is already in the
engine---the crisis-regime structure, the drawdown-conditional blend, and the
operator-configurable loss floor exist to extend the simulated distribution into
the region this panel shows lies beyond historical reach. The honest deployment is
to use the engine as calibrated here for ordinary risk measurement, and to engage
the crisis overlay when sizing for systemic scenarios.

It is worth recording what the v3.1 corrections bought, without overstating it.
The prior release's optimistic biases---winsorized tails, deletion of extreme
paths, additive drawdowns---would each have pushed realized outcomes further into
the predicted tail, lowering PIT ranks and inflating breach rates; measuring the
corrected engine is what allows the calm-year breach frequencies to land at
nominal rather than beyond it. It does not make the distribution uniform: the
corrected engine is conservative through the body, which the corrections did not,
and were not intended to, remove.

% =============================================================== 6 Limitations
\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em, itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Cross-sectional dependence.} The 62 instruments in a given year
  share a common macro environment, so the effective number of independent
  observations within a year is smaller than the cell count. The Kupiec and
  Kolmogorov--Smirnov statistics reported here assume independent cells; under the
  dependence present in the panel their $p$-values overstate precision, and the
  ``fail to reject'' verdict on non-crisis calibration should be read as
  suggestive rather than a formal size-controlled test. A year-clustered or
  block-bootstrap treatment is the correct next step and is left to future work.
  This dependence is also why the panel's power derives chiefly from its
  \emph{temporal} span across regimes, not from cross-sectional breadth.
  \item \textbf{History depth.} Price data begin in 2004. Every training window
  therefore contains the 2008 crisis, but none contains a pre-2004 regime; the
  earliest test years (from 2013) are fit on roughly nine years of data, and the
  tail estimates rest on at most $\sim$16 years of exceedances. No 1987, 2000, or
  earlier stress informs the fits, and the deep-tail parameters are correspondingly
  data-limited.
  \item \textbf{Annual horizon.} Each cell evaluates a one-year maximum drawdown.
  Shorter or path-dependent horizons would probe different aspects of the predicted
  distribution and are left to future work.
  \item \textbf{Survivorship and vendor.} The universe is composed of instruments
  that survived to the present, and all prices come from a single adjusted-close
  source; both would shift individual cells modestly without altering the aggregate
  picture.
  \item \textbf{Crisis-year labelling.} The calm/crisis split is a coarse
  stratification for interpretation; the continuous relationship in
  Table~\ref{tab:byyear} does not depend on it.
\end{enumerate}

% =============================================================== 7 Conclusion
\section{Conclusion}
Under a strict expanding-window protocol spanning 62 instruments, seven asset
classes, and twelve years---744 out-of-sample cells in total---engine release v3.1
reproduces the \emph{frequency} of ordinary tail losses on non-crisis markets: a
Kupiec test fails to reject nominal $5\%$ and $1\%$ breach rates (subject to the
cross-sectional-dependence caveat of Section~\ref{sec:limits}), while the engine
errs conservative through the body of the distribution, which a uniformity test
confirms is not itself uniform. Its behaviour under the four crisis years of the
period is fully characterised: coverage degrades monotonically with realized
severity and breaks down only at the largest systemic shocks, exactly where a
tail calibrated to prior crises must. Against four naive baselines scored by a
strictly proper rule, it is the sole model delivering \emph{calibrated and sharp}
calm-market forecasts---improving CRPS by $20$--$39\%$ in the regime where a risk
desk operates---while conceding crisis sharpness by construction, a trade the
crisis overlay exists to reverse on demand. The standard adopted throughout is the
one that matters: quantify the residual gap out of sample, state precisely what
was and was not demonstrated, and report both.
